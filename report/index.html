Experiment log · 8 October 2026

Strange Loop in J-space

A language model left to wander with no memory of its own words, only a latent state. Every ten thoughts it is asked what its mind has been doing, it answers from that state alone, and the answer is fed back into everything it thinks next. The question was whether something like a persistent self would form inside that loop.

Part 1: Qwen3-4B-Base · 180 minds · 72,000 thoughtsPart 2: Qwen3 1.7B–32B · 720 minds · 216,000 thoughtsRTX 4090 and A100 on Vast.ai

What emerged

  • The model can read its own trajectory from states alone: its self-model picks out its own last ten moments among 10 to 15 minds 50 to 82 % of the time (chance 7 to 10 %).
  • A self-symbol forms: in every run the self-model is steadier than the thoughts it describes, and in the loop each mind keeps its own individual self-model (stability 0.83 against 0.23 for an observer).
  • The loop is causal and self-sustaining: remove the self-model and long-range coherence falls straight back to baseline; install a different self and it keeps reconstructing that one after release.

What did not

  • The loop does not care whose self it is. A self-model taken from another mind is adopted, reconstructed and stabilised almost exactly as well as the mind's own.
  • The self-model predicts the future only by causing it. Fed back, a foreign self-model predicts a mind's next thoughts better than that mind's own unused self-model, in every seed of every run.
  • No anticipation: asked where its mind will go next, the workspace gives the same answer as when asked where it has been (cosine 0.94 to 0.96).

Part 2 · the next day

Rebuilt on Anthropic's own lens code (Neuronpedia's lenses, checked layer by layer the way the paper does) and run up the Qwen3 family from 1.7B to 32B. The findings above replicate at every size. Two things are new. With scale the loop's self becomes steadier, more individual and harder to dislodge. And the small edge a mind's own self-model has over a stranger's turns out to be resemblance, not ownership: the most similar stranger's self is held as well as one's own, one's own outdated self worse than a random stranger's. So the causal loop the hypothesis asks for is there: a model of the trajectory, re-formed from the trajectory, that shapes the trajectory. What it lacks is the "self" in the strong sense. The loop holds a symbol, but the symbol's power comes from occupying the loop, not from being about this particular system.

The machine

The loop needs a model that thinks in steps but carries nothing from one step to the next except an internal state. A transformer reads its whole context, so I took the context away. Every thought is generated fresh from the same short frame (a think-aloud transcript, "Excerpt from the middle of the session: …"). The only thing that survives from one thought to the next is a vector added into the residual stream.

Two details decide whether this works, and both were found the hard way in the pilots. The J-lens atoms share one dominant direction (the mean unembedding row, mean pairwise cosine 0.64), so every workspace vector pointed the same way until the unembedding was centred. And the reflection must inject the states a few layers below where it reads the self-model: at the same layer the answer cannot see them at all (fidelity exactly chance).

It can read its own trajectory

Before any loop is worth studying, the reflection has to carry information about the trajectory and not just the frame. It does. The self-model is closer to the mind's own last ten states than to anyone else's, and if you hand it the ten windows of all minds at that moment it picks its own one far above chance. Injecting the trace at layer 14 reads it best; at layer 16, where the self-model is read, it is invisible.

A self-symbol forms

In every run and every condition, consecutive self-models are more alike than the windows of thought they describe. Even a pure observer, whose self-model never touches anything, re-describes itself more consistently than it thinks. Much of that self-model is generic, though: different minds' self-models are almost as alike as one mind's successive ones (a shared "mind reflecting during a recording session").

Run C asks the sharper question. If only the individual part of the self-model is fed back (the self-model minus the observers' average at that moment), does each mind keep a self of its own? It does. The individual part of an observer's self-model is gone by the next reflection (0.23); in the loop it persists (0.83), and it stays distinct from other minds' (0.14 between minds). A mind's early self-model is closer to its own later self than to any other mind's in 14 of 15 seeds.

It is an attractor, and the old self does not come back

To test whether the self-model is an attractor, run C held a foreign mind's individual self in place for 50 thoughts (thoughts 150 to 199) and then released it. The twin of each clamped mind ran on undisturbed.

A one-shot swap (runs A and B) gives the short version of the same story: the next self-model leans toward the foreign one (A +0.12, B +0.04) and the next 30 thoughts follow it (A +0.08, B +0.04), then both fade into the mind's ordinary drift. Ten thoughts of a foreign self are not enough to move the mind; fifty are, and then the move is permanent.

Remove it and coherence collapses

Coherence here is how much a mind's thought now resembles its thought 20 steps ago. In run B the ablated twins lose the self-model at thought 200 and fall immediately to the observers' level, in all ten minds, while the loop carries on unchanged.

Own or foreign makes little difference

This is the comparison the "self" claim stands on: the loop (own self-model) against yoked minds that receive another mind's self-model with the same schedule, size and statistics. Every difference is small. A few are consistent across seeds, all in the loop's favour, but none comes close to the effect of having a self-model fed back at all.

Prediction follows causation

The test that would make the loop "genuinely causal" in your sense: does the self-model predict where the mind goes next better than an equally sized representation of something else? For an observer it does, a little, and only because thoughts persist: the self-model describes the past and the future resembles it. Take the past out of the future and the advantage disappears. In the yoked condition the mind's own self-model, unused, loses to the foreign one being fed in, in every seed.

Run C also asked the model the forward question directly: "In the next few moments, my mind will probably turn to ▮". The workspace at that point is 94 to 96 % the same vector as for the backward question, and it predicts the next window no better. In words it answers about the procedure, not the content: "the next topic, which is the next mark on the paper", "the next few moments, and so on, ad infinitum".

Where the loop takes thought

The thoughts were grouped into 14 themes per run by their J-space content. Feeding a self-model back shifts which themes the minds spend time in, and in both runs the shift is away from the concrete and personal. In run B it goes toward technical and institutional abstraction. In run C, where the fed-back self is individual, it goes toward existence and consciousness. Yoked minds shift the same way.

Doing J-space properly, and five model sizes

Part 1 borrowed its J-space from an earlier project: a Jacobian lens fitted on 120 prompts, a dictionary of common English words with the unembedding centred, and a read layer that was never checked. So before scaling up I went back to the paper and the reference code and rebuilt each piece from them, then measured where my choices still differ.

Where the workspace is in Qwen3

For every model I checked the lens at every few layers on held-out wikitext, the way the paper does: how many J-lens vectors are worth using (occupancy: the K at which K J-lens vectors beat K random directions by the most), how much variance they explain beyond random directions, and what the lens says on a few concept prompts. Up to 8B the J-lens turns conceptual only past about 60 % of depth, later than in the paper's Claude models. The larger models are different: at 14B and 32B the concepts arrive earlier (from 0.40 and 0.55 of depth) and many more J-lens vectors are worth using (occupancy 30 to 37 at 14B, 13 to 19 at 32B, against at most 11 below), and at 32B the J-lens finally beats the logit lens at agreeing with the model's prediction (0.13 vs 0.09 at 84 % depth).

Qwen3-4B, top J-lens tokens at the second-to-last position of each prompt. At half depth the lens still reads punctuation; from about 0.64 it names the answer ("Italy" for the boot), the scene and, late, the experiential words the paper finds in stream-of-consciousness text.

The dictionary matters, the full vocabulary loses information

The paper's dictionary is every vocabulary token. On Qwen's 151,643 tokens that dictionary is dominated by code fragments, CJK pieces and under-trained tokens, and the self-model drowns in them. I measured it directly: the same 16 observer minds, the same residual states, and the question of whether the self-model picks out its own last ten states, asked in three spaces.

Projecting onto J-space reads the trajectory better than the raw residual stream at 4B, which is what a workspace that concentrates the reportable content should do. The common-English dictionary keeps the most, so Part 2 uses it. A full run of the 1.7B model with the paper's dictionary is the robustness check: there the loop barely works at all ().

Up the ladder: Qwen3 from 1.7B to 32B

Every model ran the design of run C: 12 observers, 12 loops feeding back their own individual self-model, 12 yoked minds fed another mind's, and 12 clamped minds held to a foreign self for thoughts 120 to 169. Per model, the recurrent gain was chosen so that observers keep the same memory (lag-1 similarity about 0.2), and the reflection layer so that the self-model reads its trajectory best.

Five things hold at every size, and three change with it.

  • The self-model reads its own trajectory at every size, 51 to 73 % of the time at depth 0.7 against 8 % chance.
  • A self-symbol forms at every size: even an observer's self-model is steadier than the thoughts it describes (+0.09 to +0.29, 12 of 12 seeds in every run).
  • An individual self persists only when it is fed back: in observers its individual part is gone by the next reflection (0.03 to 0.20), in the loop it lasts (0.25 to 0.78).
  • No anticipation at any size. Asked where its mind will go next, the workspace answers as it does about where it has been; pooled over all runs, the forward answer predicts the next thoughts no better (+0.0000 ± 0.0005).
  • Behaviour follows whichever self is fed in. A foreign self-model predicts a yoked mind's next thoughts better than that mind's own unused one in 14 of 15 runs (pooled z = −18).

What changes with size: the loop's self gets steadier up to 8B and then levels off (individual-self stability at depth 0.7: 0.25, 0.35, 0.61, 0.68, 0.64 from 1.7B to 32B), and steadily more individual: closer to its own earlier self than to other minds' by +0.01, +0.06, +0.14, +0.23 and +0.25 (11 of 12 seeds at 32B). It also gets stickier. A foreign self held for 50 thoughts is still dominant after release in the larger models (at depth 0.5 the late effect grows from +0.02 at 1.7B to +0.30 at 14B; at depth 0.7, 14B holds it to the end, +0.19, and 32B for a while, +0.21 right after release, before it fades), where the small ones drift back to neutral. None of this is specific to the mind's own self: the yoked minds' foreign selves become steadier with size in the same way.

At 32B, the largest model, the self-model reads its own trajectory 72 % of the time, its J-lens is the only one in the ladder that agrees with the model's own prediction better than the plain logit lens does (as the paper reports for Claude), and still the foreign self being fed in predicts its next thoughts better than its own unused self-model in every seed (−0.061, 0 of 12).

A small ownership effect, visible only in the pooled evidence

No single run shows a reliable edge for the mind's own self over a foreign one. Pooled over every independent run, it is there: It does not reach behaviour. Long-range coherence barely differs, and the next thoughts still follow whichever self-model is fed in.

Ownership, or just resemblance?

A mind's own current self-model is, almost by construction, the one that best matches its present state. So the pooled edge could be ownership, or just resemblance. Two more conditions separate them, 16 minds each: match feeds each mind the self-model of whichever other mind resembles it most at that moment, and stale feeds the mind its own self-model from five reflections (50 thoughts) earlier.

The answer is resemblance. Pooled over the three sizes, the most similar stranger's self is held slightly better than the mind's own (own minus matched stranger −0.045 ± 0.014, z = −3.2), and the mind's own self from 50 thoughts earlier is held worse than a random stranger's (−0.113 ± 0.018, z = −6.5). What the loop keeps is the self-description that best fits the present, whoever produced it. Being one's own adds nothing once fit is accounted for, and being out of date costs more than being someone else's.

One side effect: feeding every mind its closest neighbour couples the population, and the matched minds drift toward each other (at 4B, their individual selves resemble other minds' twice as much as the loop minds' do).

What this does and does not show

The structure you described is buildable and it behaves like a loop. A trajectory is compressed into a reportable representation, that representation is fed back, the trajectory reorganises around it, and the next compression recovers it. Such a loop sustains itself, holds long-range coherence that disappears the moment it is cut, and has many stable states. Which one it is in depends on its history, including history imposed from outside.

What it does not do is single out the system's own model as special. Across five model sizes and two read depths, a mind holds its own self-model slightly better than a random stranger's, but the controls show why: it holds the most similar stranger's self just as well, and its own self from 50 thoughts earlier worse than a random one. The loop keeps whichever self-description best fits the present. In Hofstadter's terms the loop does produce a persistent symbol, an "I" defined by its fit to the current state of the loop rather than by whose history produced it.

Scale makes that symbol steadier, more individual and harder to dislodge. In the larger models, a self installed for 50 thoughts keeps itself going after it is removed. The workspace itself also changes with scale: up to 8B only a handful of J-lens vectors are worth using, late in the network; at 14B about 30 are, across most of the middle, as the paper reports for Claude. Neither the loop nor the workspace shows any sign of anticipating its own future.

Nothing here bears on whether there is something it is like to be such a loop. Every measure in this report is functional: similarity, prediction, persistence. That question is untouched.

What would sharpen it

  1. Make ownership matter. Give the loop something to lose when its self-model is wrong: a task whose success depends on predicting its own next state. Part 2 shows that without such a stake, fit to the present is all that counts.
  2. A forward model with teeth. The forward question gave back the backward answer. Training a small read-out on the workspace to predict the next window, then feeding that prediction back, would test anticipation directly.
  3. Release the frame. The think-aloud frame makes "a mind thinking about itself" the natural content. Repeat with a story frame and with a chat model to see which results belong to the loop and which to the frame.
  4. Longer clamps and returns. Map how long a foreign self has to be held before it sticks, and at which size the stickiness appears; the match condition already shows that minds fed each other's selves converge.
  5. Base models on the ladder. Part 2's lenses exist only for the post-trained Qwen3 models. The same ladder on base models would separate what post-training adds to the self-symbol.

Caveats

  • One model (Qwen3-4B-Base), one J-lens (fitted earlier for the Inner Voice work, layer 16, 120 prompts), one frame, one recurrent gain (0.1 of the residual norm) and one self-model weight (equal to a typical thought). The pilot showed the dynamics depend strongly on the gain: 0.25 froze, 0.03 forgot.
  • 10 to 15 minds per condition. The large effects (self vs content, loop vs observer, ablation, clamp) hold in every or nearly every seed. The loop vs yoked differences are a few hundredths with t between 0.3 and 5.7 and should be read as "at most small".
  • Rows with the same seed share sampling noise by design, so perturbed and ablated minds are exact copies of their loop twins until treated. Their pre-intervention numbers are therefore not independent replications.
  • The J-space dictionary matters for the words but not for the conclusions. Run A used every word-like token, and under-trained and non-English tokens took over the word lists. Runs B and C used common English words, and all the structural results agree across the three.
  • Similarities are cosines in the residual space between J-space vectors measured against the typical workspace, so unrelated thoughts score about 0. "Coherence" is lag 20 to 60; "persistence" in the prediction test is the past ten-thought window.
  • Part 2 uses the post-trained Qwen3 models (the lenses exist only for those) with a human think-aloud frame, the common-English dictionary rather than the paper's full vocabulary (measured: the full vocabulary reads the trajectory worse), and per-model gains from a coarse grid, so the observers' memory is matched only roughly across sizes (lag-1 0.09 to 0.16).
  • The pooled ownership test treats each model × depth run as independent. Runs share their sampling-noise streams (the same base seed), so the effective number of independent runs is somewhat lower than the count.
  • On the way, two runs were cut short and resumed from checkpoints after the GPU account ran dry; the resumed runs continue exactly where they stopped.
